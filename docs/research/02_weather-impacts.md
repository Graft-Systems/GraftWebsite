# Weather Impacts on Powdery and Downy Mildew Progression

---

## Summary

Weather is the primary driver of powdery mildew (*Erysiphe necator*, PM) and downy mildew (*Plasmopara viticola*, DM) epidemics in vineyards worldwide. The two pathogens respond to overlapping but distinct meteorological windows: PM is predominantly temperature-controlled and does **not** require free water (conidia germinate at RH ≥40%), whereas DM is a wet-climate disease whose entire infection and sporulation cycle requires liquid water or RH ≥80%. Both pathogens are suppressed by extreme heat and by solar UV radiation, but through different mechanisms. Quantified thresholds are well-established for both pathogens and form the direct mathematical inputs to the Gubler-Thomas Risk Index, the Mills Table for ascospore infection, DMCast, and the EPI/Goidanich models (see Category 6 for model architectures). Climate projections for priority regions show diverging trajectories: in Napa/Sonoma and Burgundy, rising spring temperatures are likely to accelerate epidemic onset for DM, but the net effect on spray requirements depends heavily on concurrent precipitation trends.

## Key Findings

| Parameter | Powdery Mildew (*E. necator*) | Downy Mildew (*P. viticola*) |
|---|---|---|
| **Optimal temperature** | 23–26°C | 21–25°C |
| **Functional range** | 6–32°C | 4–30°C |
| **Conidial/sporangia germination optimum** | 25°C | 20–21°C |
| **No germination below/above** | <5°C, >35°C | <4°C, >30.2°C (infection) |
| **Lethal temperature** | ≥36°C for ≥4 h (sublethal); ≥38°C for ≥2 h (revised GT threshold) | ≥42.8°C (single day, permanent) |
| **Free water requirement** | None (conidia; detrimental at leaf level) | Yes — zoospore release requires free water |
| **RH for infection** | ≥40%; optimum ~85%; germination falls above 87% | ≥80% (sporulation trigger); ≥90% (oospore maturation) |
| **Leaf wetness (infection)** | ~12–15 h @ 10–15°C (ascospores); not required for conidia | ≥2 h @ 21°C to ~14 h @ 4°C (zoospore infection) |
| **Primary inoculum source** | Chasmothecia (ascospores) + flag-shoot mycelium | Oospores in soil/leaf litter |
| **Primary infection trigger** | Precipitation ≥2–3 mm + T >10°C + leaf wetness | Rain >2.5–10 mm + T >10–11°C + shoots >10 cm |
| **Latent period** | 5 days @ 26°C; up to 25 days @ 9°C | 5–18 days (T-dependent) |
| **Sporulation timing** | Asexual conidia, continuous if conditions met | Nocturnal: night MP ≥3 h, T 10–30°C |
| **Solar UV suppression** | Strong direct effect — UV-B extends latent period, kills colonies | Indirect (host-mediated); direct UV inhibits zoospores |
| **Gubler-Thomas trigger** | 3 consecutive days ≥6 h between 21–30°C | (DM: 3-10 rule equivalent) |
| **Risk index units** | +20 pts/day when ≥6 h @ 21–30°C; –10 pts/day otherwise; –10 if T ≥35°C | N/A (separate DM models) |

## Detailed Notes

### Temperature Thresholds — Powdery Mildew

**Germination and growth range:**
- Conidial germination occurs across 6–32°C; no germination at ≤5°C or ≥35°C [S1, S2].
- Optimum germination temperature: **25°C** (maximum germination 83.69% after 24 h) [S1].
- Optimum germination RH: **80–85%** (82.67% germination at 80% RH) [S1].
- Colony growth and sporulation optimal at **23–30°C**, with absolute optimum near **26°C** [S3].
- Disease development on *Vitis vinifera* cv. Chardonnay, Carmenere, Merlot increases linearly from **6 to 23°C** [S4].
- During favorable temperature periods, latent period (spore germination → new colony sporulation) is **as short as 5 days** at optimal temperature (~26°C) [S3].
- At 9°C, latent period ≥25 days [S3].
- Incubation period: 13–14 days at 20–23°C; 19–24 days at 10°C; >23 days at 6°C [S4].

**High-temperature suppression and lethality (revised Gubler-Thomas thresholds):**
- At **30–34°C**: growth and reproduction continue but are sub-lethal; long exposures (up to 24 h) reduce reproduction [S5].
- At **36°C for 4 h**: 100% of colonies still grew and sporulated (growth delayed 1.2 days) [S5].
- At **36°C for 6 h**: 94% of colonies still grew and sporulated (growth delayed 1.7 days) [S5].
- At **36°C for ≥10 h**: substantial hyphal mortality occurs [S3].
- At **38°C for 2 h**: lethal effects significant — this is the **revised Gubler-Thomas high-temperature threshold** (equal or better disease control vs. original 35°C threshold, with 5 fewer applications over 2 years) [S5, S6].
- At **40°C for 4.1 h**: predicted total loss of spore viability (0% germination) [S5].
- At **42°C for 2.0 h**: lethal for spore production [S5].
- At **44°C for 0.5–1.1 h**: lethal for germination, spore production, colony growth [S5].
- The fungus can **recover from sublethal high-temperature exposures** when conditions return to favorable; repeated short exposures (e.g., 3× 4 h at 36°C) do not accumulate to same effect as one 12-h exposure [S6].

**Gubler-Thomas Risk Index (GT) — temperature inputs precisely:**
- **Trigger**: 3 consecutive days with ≥6 h at 21–30°C (70–85°F) — if not met, index resets to zero [S7, S8].
- **+20 points/day** if ≥6 h between 21.1°C and 29.4°C (70–85°F) [S7].
- **–10 points/day** if <6 h between 21.1°C and 29.4°C [S7].
- **–10 points/day** if maximum daily temperature ≥35°C (95°F) [S7].
- Index capped at 100; floor at 0.
- Index 0–30: low risk (spray at label maximum interval) [S8].
- Index 40–50: moderate risk (intermediate intervals) [S8].
- Index 60–100: high risk (label minimum intervals: sulfur 7 days, DMIs 14 days) [S8].

**Cold-temperature effects:**
- Brief exposure (2–8 h) to 2–4°C post-inoculation reduces infection efficiency, increases latent period, causes hyphal mortality [S3].
- Extended periods at 9°C push latent period to ≥25 days [S3].

### Temperature Thresholds — Downy Mildew

**Infection range:**
- Infection range: **4.0–30.2°C**; optimal infection temperature: **21.0°C** [S9].
- Shortest wetness duration for infection at optimal temperature: **2 h** [S9].
- Spore germination and infection 13–30°C (Cornell IPM range); 55–86°F (13–30°C) per Cornell Extension [S10].
- Growth optimum: **25°C** (Plasmopara general) [S11].

**Sporulation:**
- Sporulation occurs **at night** (sunlight inhibits sporangiophore emergence).
- Sporulation trigger (night moist period, MP): temperature **10–30°C** + nighttime moist period **≥3 h** [S9].
- Moist hour defined as: RH ≥80%, OR rain >0 mm, OR leaf wetness >30 min [S9].
- Sporangia production rate increases up to **23°C**; >29°C: no sporulation (Pessl METOS model) [S12].
- Accumulated 50°C-hours triggers completion of sporulation — equivalent to 4 h at 13°C or 3 h at 17°C [S12].
- Sporangia die rapidly when VPD is high (drying conditions); RH <50% resets sporulation counter [S12].

**Primary infection thresholds (oospore-triggered):**
- Classical European **3-10 rule** (Baldacci 1947): temperature ≥**10°C** + shoots ≥**10 cm** + rainfall ≥**10 mm** within 24–48 h [S13].
- Kennelly et al. 2007 field-validated threshold: rain >**2.5 mm** + temperature >**11°C** + E&L phenology stage **>12** — consistent across 15 years of data [S14].
- North American 10-10-10 rule (practical): >10°C + >1/10 inch rain + >10 cm shoot growth [S11].
- Australian 10-10-24 model: >10°C + >10 mm rain in **24 h** [S13].
- Shoot tissue susceptible as early as E&L stage 5; oospore germination (not host susceptibility) is the limiting step for disease onset [S14].
- Oospores continue to germinate and cause primary infections throughout the growing season — not depleted at bloom as historically assumed [S14].

**Oospore maturation conditions:**
- Maturation requires mild, rainy spring days: rainfall ≥6 mm + T 6–26°C + RH >90% [S13].
- <5–10 mm rain over 3 weeks + low early-spring temperatures stops maturation [S13].
- Oospores reach maximum germination at **20°C** (optimal germination temperature) [S15].
- Oospores complete maturation period at ~120 days from overwintering onset (DFO₅₀); germination peaks at/before grapevine sprouting, showing host-pathogen phenological synchrony [S15].
- Key drivers of maturation rate: cumulative temperature sum (SOT), time from overwintering start (DFO), cumulative rainfall [S15].
- Oospore germination inhibited above **32°C** [S15].
- Oospore germinability decreases as DFO increases — older oospores (late season) germinate less readily [S15].

**High-temperature eradicative effects:**
- A single day at **42.8°C** permanently reduced sporulation from existing (non-incubating) lesions to trace levels, even after conditions returned to favorable [S14].

### Leaf Wetness Duration

**Powdery mildew — ascospore infection (Modified Mills Table, UC IPM model):**
The model uses 2/3 of the original Mills (apple scab) leaf wetness values:

| Daily Avg Temp (°F/°C) | Hours LW for Heavy Ascospore Infection (2/3 Mills) | Full Mills Value |
|---|---|---|
| 42°F / 5.6°C | 40 h | 60 h |
| 48–49°F / 8.9°C | 20 h | 30 h |
| 54–55°F / 12.8°C | 16 h | 24 h |
| 63–75°F / 17–24°C | 12 h | 18 h |
| 77°F / 25°C | 14 h | 21 h |
| 78°F / 25.6°C | 17.3 h | 26 h |

Key rules [S8]:
- At 55°F (12.8°C): **16 h** LW for heavy ascospore infection.
- At 65°F (18.3°C): **12 h** LW for heavy infection.
- Once ascospore infection occurs, the model **switches to the conidial risk-index phase**.

**Powdery mildew — conidial infection:**
- Conidia do **not** require leaf wetness for germination; free water at leaf surface is **detrimental** (causes conidial lysis) [S3, S4].
- Leaf wetness >8 h in Pessl METOS model: index **decreases by 10 points** (Ampelomyces quisqualis antagonism mechanism) [S12].

**Downy mildew — zoospore infection (leaf wetness duration):**
- Minimum infection period: **2 h** at optimal temperature 21°C [S9].
- At 4°C: infection requires very long wetness; marginal at lower end of range.
- At 30.2°C: upper limit for infection; above this, infection ceases [S9].
- Wetness period (WP) defined as uninterrupted leaf wetness OR interrupted by ≤1 h maximum [S9].
- For primary infection from oospores: ≥2.5 mm rain required; following rain, RH must not drop below 70% for sporangia to develop within **16–24 h** depending on temperature [S12].
- Continuous rain of **5 mm** interpreted as a "strong rainfall" spreading zoospores (Pessl METOS model) [S12].

**Downy mildew — incubation and symptom appearance:**
- Latent (incubation) period: **5–18 days** depending on temperature [S16].
- At 23°C: incubation period = **4 days** [S9].
- Symptom onset typically **7–12 days post-infection** (Cornell) [S10].
- Incubation progress calculated daily as function of temperature and RH (Orlandini et al. 2008 equation); must reach 100% for symptoms to appear [S9].
- Fruit resistant to downy mildew **3–4 weeks after bloom**; cluster stems remain susceptible all season [S10].

### Humidity, VPD, Dew Point

**Powdery mildew:**
- Operates across RH 40–100%; **optimum ~85% RH** [S3, S17].
- At RH 83–86.5%: optimum for colony development on leaves (Carroll & Wilcox 2003, at 23–26°C) [S17].
- Conidial germination frequency rises linearly with RH up to 84%, then **falls sharply above 87% RH** (split-point: theoretical optimum 87.4% RH = 371 Pa VPD) [S17].
- VPD range for optimal conidial germination: **61–1914 Pa** (corresponding to 98–39% RH) [S17].
- Below ~40% RH: conidia can still germinate (critical difference from DM) — germination documented at RH as low as 33–35% at 20°C [S4].
- High humidity (approaching 100% or free water): **detrimental** — conidia lyse [S3].
- Humidity indirectly drives PM through: (a) reduced UV exposure in cloudy/humid conditions; (b) canopy shading effects; (c) dense canopy microclimate creating local RH elevations [S18].

**Downy mildew:**
- Sporulation trigger requires RH ≥80% (or rain, or leaf wetness >30 min) for "moist hour" classification [S9].
- Oospore maturation: RH >90% required [S13].
- Sporangia mortality (attached to sporangiophores): modeled as function of **VPD** — higher VPD = faster mortality [S9].
- Sporangia mortality (detached/airborne): also VPD-dependent; VPD of 9.82 kPa (season avg 2016) vs. 12.12 kPa (2015) and 13.25 kPa (2017) corresponded to more favorable vs. less favorable DM seasons [S9].
- When RH drops below 50%: sporangia counter reset to 0 — sporulation stops [S12].
- Spore trap studies from Spain: P. viticola counts most strongly correlated with **humidity, dew point, and average temperature** [S13].
- Spring spore abundance in Bordeaux vineyards (2010–2017): **disease onset most significantly correlated with cumulative spring precipitation** [S13].

**VPD as a unified predictor:**
VPD integrates temperature and humidity into a single metric that more accurately captures evaporative potential and host-surface conditions than either parameter alone. For DM, VPD directly governs sporangial viability post-production. For PM, VPD (via RH) modulates conidial germination probability and colony expansion rate.

### Rainfall and Splash Dispersal

**Downy mildew — primary inoculum:**
- Rain splash is the **primary dispersal mechanism** for oospore-derived zoospores from leaf litter to lower leaf surfaces [S19].
- Rainfall ≥2.5–10 mm required to trigger primary infection depending on model (Kennelly: >2.5 mm; 3-10 rule: 10 mm in 24–48 h) [S14, S13].
- Rossi & Caffi (2012) quantified: rain disperses primary inoculum from leaf litter, with sporangial dose reaching leaves proportional to rainfall intensity and duration [S19].

**Downy mildew — secondary inoculum:**
- Sporangia dispersed by both **rain splash** (Kast 1997) and **wind** (Diaz et al. 1997, 1998) [S9].
- Sporangia release 6–8 zoospores each when deposited on wet host surfaces [S9].
- Most sporangia die during **daylight hours** in fair weather immediately following nocturnal production [S14].
- Over 50% of sporangia still viable after 12–24 h under **overcast conditions** [S14].

**Powdery mildew — chasmothecia dispersal:**
- Chasmothecia dispersed to vine bark and leaf litter by **rain**, also by extreme winds [S3].
- Ascospore release requires **precipitation ≥2–3 mm** coincident with T >10°C [S3].
- In mild climates (California, Italy, South Australia), significant chasmothecia survival in leaf litter through winter; in cold-winter regions (NY State), survival confined to vine bark [S3].
- Ascospore infection model uses rainfall trigger: ≥2.5 mm rainfall + 8–12 h leaf wetness at 10–15°C (Pessl METOS ascospore model) [S12].
- Free water (rain, dew, irrigation) initiates circumscissal dehiscence of chasmothecia, releasing ascospores into air currents [S3].

**Rain as a *negative* factor for PM conidia:**
- Free water lyses conidia on leaf surfaces — inoculated leaves exposed to rain at 1 h or 72 h post-inoculation markedly reduced PM development [S4].
- PM is distinctively a "dry-climate" disease in its conidial stage; epidemics continue through California's dry summers without rainfall [S20].

### Wind, Solar Radiation, Other

**Wind:**
- DM: wind speed >9.0 m/s strongly associated with sporulation events and spore dispersal [S13].
- PM: wind ≥2.3 m/s sufficient for conidial dispersal; conidia are passively airborne at ambient wind speeds [S13].
- Row orientation affects within-canopy wind speed: North-South oriented rows show lowest diurnal wind velocities; East-West and NW-SE rows preserve more wind penetration, reducing canopy RH [S21].
- Dense canopies reduce wind velocity to **10–20% of ambient** [S22]; this extends leaf wetness duration and elevates canopy RH.
- Higher within-canopy wind velocity → faster drying after dew/rain → reduced DM and *B. cinerea* risk [S21].

**Solar radiation and UV:**
- Sunlight inhibits PM through two synergistic mechanisms: **(i) UV-B radiation** damages surface colonies; **(ii) elevated leaf surface temperatures** exceed growth optima [S18, S23].
- At exposed leaf surfaces, temperatures can reach 30°F (17°C) above air temperature on sunny days, pushing local temperatures well above the 35–38°C lethal range [S23].
- **Effect of UV-B on latent period** (Austin & Wilcox 2012, Cornell):
  - At 68°F (20°C): 4 daily 6-h UV-B doses extend latent period by ~2 days [S18].
  - At 86°F (30°C): 4 daily 6-h UV-B doses extend latent period from 7 to **15 days** [S18].
- Shading experiments: 1-layer shade cloth (45% natural light) → PM severity 2–3× greater than full sunlight; 2-layer (20% natural light) → further increase [S18].
- Natural shading in Chardonnay (next to treeline): **8–40-fold** increase in PM severity on interior leaves vs. exterior leaves [S18].
- UV-filtered sunlight: fruit PM severity **2–5-fold higher** than unfiltered sunlight [S18].
- Inhibitory UV-B effects are significantly greater at 30°C than at 20 or 25°C — synergistic heat-UV interaction [S24].
- DM: sunlight inhibits sporangiophore emergence — **all sporulation occurs at night** [S9].
- UV-C (254 nm) applications at 200 J/m² reduce *E. necator* conidial germination to nil [S25].
- UV-C at 100–200 J/m² applied weekly suppresses PM on fruit and foliage equivalent to standard fungicide programs in field conditions [S25].

**Dew point:**
- Dew formation on leaf surfaces at night provides the moisture needed for DM nocturnal sporulation (RH ≥80% trigger often reached via dew) [S10].
- Even without rainfall, heavy dew nights are sufficient to fuel DM sporulation; morning dew generates fresh sporangia that spread rapidly when tissues rewet [S20].
- P. viticola spore counts in Spain most correlated with **dew point and average temperature** (above precipitation) [S13].

### Microclimate & Canopy Effects

**Canopy density:**
- Dense canopies increase local RH by **up to 10 percentage points** above ambient (transpiration effect) [S22].
- Wind velocity inside dense canopies reduced to **10–20% of ambient** [S22].
- In VSP systems: denser canopies increase leaf predisposition to P. viticola infection and enhance nighttime canopy moisture — **however, canopy density alone does not necessarily drive more severe DM epidemics** when temperature and rainfall govern the overall season [S26].
- Canopy density effect on PM: disease incidence increases significantly from single to double to four-cordon configurations [S27].
- High canopy sunlight (open canopies) reduces DM spread under grass cover; for PM, canopy light and hygrothermal conditions are most important drivers [S28].
- Soil tillage (vs. cover crops) associated with higher PM and DM infection percentages; disease risk better predicted by leaf area, leaf nitrogen, and canopy density than by weather alone [S28].

**Row orientation:**
- East-West oriented rows: **higher morning relative humidity** in bunch zone despite higher daytime wind velocity [S21].
- North-South orientation: lowest diurnal wind velocity within work rows — most at-risk for humidity accumulation if winds are primarily meridional [S21].
- Prevailing wind direction is the dominant factor; row orientation effectiveness depends on terrain and local wind regime [S21].
- Recommendation: orient rows toward prevailing wind direction to maximize drying after dew/rain events [S21].

**Slope and elevation:**
- Higher elevation typically means cooler temperatures, potentially extending favorable PM/DM infection windows in spring/autumn but reducing summer disease pressure due to lower average temperatures.
- Slope aspect affects solar radiation interception and canopy temperature: south-facing slopes (Northern Hemisphere) receive more direct radiation → higher UV exposure → reduced PM colonization on sun-exposed surfaces.
- Cold air drainage on slopes reduces overnight dew duration by keeping air moving, reducing DM sporulation opportunity.

**Training systems:**
- Vertical shoot positioning (VSP): more open canopy than sprawling systems; better UV penetration and air movement reduces PM and DM pressure [S23].
- Y-trellis vs. VSP in southern Brazil: Y-trellis associated with reduced DM and *Botrytis* bunch rot incidence [S29].
- Higher training/taller trunks: create cooler, more humid microclimate for grapes, potentially increasing DM/fungal disease pressure [S30].
- Rain shelter over rows: **delays DM disease occurrence by 21–28 days** and reduces final disease index by 82–83% [S31].

### Climate Change Impacts by Priority Region

#### Napa & Sonoma (Priority 1)

- Napa Valley growing season has advanced by **nearly one month** vs. 1950s baseline (Scripps/Cayan study cited by industry sources) — earlier budbreak expands the vulnerable window for both PM and DM [S32].
- Napa 2024 GDD: 13% higher than 2023; Howell Mountain/Aetna Springs reached >4,000 GDD (Winkler Region V) [S32].
- Heat events (July 2024) with Calistoga reaching 114°F (45.5°C) — above the 42.8°C eradicative threshold for DM sporangia — may temporarily suppress secondary DM cycles [S32].
- **PM trajectory (Napa/Sonoma)**: Extended warm springs favor conidia epidemic development; warmer winters reduce chasmothecia overwinter mortality and increase flag-shoot inoculum [S33].
- **DM trajectory (Napa/Sonoma)**: Napa/Sonoma remain relatively low DM pressure (dry Mediterranean summers); risk concentrated in fog-influenced areas (Carneros, Russian River Valley, Petaluma Gap) where marine influence keeps RH high overnight [S34].
- Milder winters → pathogen inoculum not killed at historical rates → increased baseline inoculum for both PM and DM in spring [S33].
- Warmer winters → earlier budbreak → longer vulnerable period → potentially more infection events per season [S33].
- Powdery Mildew Dashboard (Napa Valley Vintners / Western Weather Group) tracks daily GT stress activation hours by AVA in real time [S35].
- CIMIS (California Irrigation Management Information System) provides free hourly weather data including leaf wetness and VPD statewide [S35].

#### Burgundy (Priority 2)

- Zito et al. (2018) Burgundy study (400 growers, 1995–2014, 5 sub-regions): spray applications for mildews **significantly related to monthly climate** — **negative coefficient for May/June temperatures** (warmer = fewer treatments) and **positive coefficient for April/May rainfall** (more rain = more treatments) [S36].
- Projected reduction in spray applications under RCP 8.5: **−4.8% to −34.1%** by end of 21st century [S36].
- By 2046–2065: −1.8 to −2.4 sprays/season; by 2081–2100: −3.6 to −4.9 sprays/season (varies by sub-region) [S36].
- Mâconnais and Côte Chalonnaise (southernmost): strongest projected reduction in spray numbers (greater temperature rise projected) [S36].
- Chablisien (northernmost): smallest projected reduction (cooler base temperatures mean smaller absolute temperature increase effect) [S36].
- Counteracting forces: increasing temperatures shorten DM incubation, enabling **more infection cycles per season** even if total rainfall decreases; Salinari et al. (2006) found temperature increases **more than offset** precipitation reductions for DM pressure [S37].
- Models project slight spring/early summer rainfall increase in some GCMs for Burgundy — this would increase DM primary infection risk, partially countering temperature-driven spray reduction [S36].

#### Bordeaux (Priority 3)

- Bordeaux faces significant mildew pressure due to Atlantic-influenced humidity and spring/early summer rainfall [S34].
- Chen et al. 2019 (commercial vineyards, 2010–2017): **DM onset timing strongly correlated with cumulative spring precipitation** — pre-programmed calendar sprays need revision in light of inter-annual rainfall variability [S13].
- 2024 Bordeaux vintage: elevated rainfall during growing season → higher mildew pressure → some organic growers required non-organic sprays; others needed more copper sprays (copper buildup concerns) [S34].
- 26% of Bordeaux now certified organic — making copper-only DM management more important and weather-model guidance more economically critical [S34].
- Climate projections: temperature increases advance DM epidemic onset; mildews expected to remain major phytosanitary threat [S11].

#### Mendoza (Priority 4)

- Mendoza's climate (semi-arid, high altitude, large diurnal thermal amplitude) naturally limits DM — low summer rainfall (most precipitation as snow/winter) means moisture for sporulation is episodic rather than continuous.
- Night temperatures frequently fall below 10°C in higher-elevation zones (Upper Valle de Uco), suppressing DM sporulation [S38].
- PM remains the primary fungal concern in Mendoza; semi-arid conditions with RH ≥40% sufficient for conidial germination, and warm days (21–30°C) match the GT risk zone [S38].
- Brazilian climate-change modeling (Hamada et al. 2017): rising temperatures increase DM latent period and reduce infection severity, but extend the season window — **net effect depends on precipitation regime** [S39].
- At high elevation (Gualtallary Alto, >1,400 m), significant spring rainfall plus post-rainfall humidity creates defined DM infection windows; irrigation in lower Mendoza can create localized wetness events analogous to rainfall [S38].

#### Global Context

- Global survey (Bois, Zito, Calonnec 2017, n = many respondents worldwide): mildews are the #1–#3 disease in **virtually all grape-growing climates** [S40].
- DM reported as #1 disease where growing season GST = 14.9–20.3°C + GSR = 184–727 mm [S40].
- PM reported as #1 in a wider temperature range (15.1–21.8°C) and **lower rainfall** (59–675 mm) — confirming PM's lower moisture dependence [S40].
- Under RCP 4.5 projections to 2050: mildews retain status as major phytosanitary threat globally; distribution shifts poleward and to higher elevations [S40].

### Disease Biology & Lifecycle Drivers

#### *Erysiphe necator* (Powdery Mildew)

**Overwintering:**
- **Two overwintering strategies** (genotype-linked):
  - **Genotype A**: Survives as **mycelium in dormant infected lateral buds** → gives rise to "flag shoots" in spring; more common in mild-winter climates (California, Italy, southern France) [S41].
  - **Genotype B**: Survives predominantly as **chasmothecia (cleistothecia)** — principal inoculum in colder regions (New York, parts of Europe) [S41].
- In California and similar mild-winter climates, a **significant proportion of chasmothecia are released during autumn or winter rains** before budbreak [S3].
- Chasmothecia survive on vine bark (cold-winter regions) or within leaf litter (mild winters) — **naked chasmothecia do not survive winter in soil** [S3].

**Chasmothecia maturation:**
- Morphological maturity: ~4 weeks after initiation during mid-to-late summer [S3].
- Physiological maturity (ascospore viability): may take **several months**, especially in cold climates [S3].
- Each chasmothecium contains 4–6 asci, each with 4–7 ascospores (15–25 µm × 10–14 µm) [S3].
- Initiation within 24 h of contact between compatible mating types at optimal temperatures [S3].

**Ascospore infection:**
- Ascospores wind-dispersed after chasmothecia dehisce on contact with rain/dew/irrigation [S3].
- Requires precipitation ≥2–3 mm + T >10°C for release [S3].
- Germination and infection can proceed with subsequent drying — continued wetness NOT required for ascospore infection (unlike DM zoospores) [S3].
- Mills Table (modified at 2/3 threshold for heavy infection) governs ascospore infection period decisions [S8].

**Conidial (asexual) cycle:**
- Conidia produced continuously in chains by sporulating colonies during growing season.
- Wind-dispersed; minimum wind ≥2.3 m/s for liftoff [S13].
- Conidia viable in dry conditions (RH ≥40%) — survive and infect without free water [S3].
- New colony sporulates in as few as **5 days** at 26°C.
- Colony density self-limiting: high inoculum density (>10 spores/mm²) does not further shorten latent period [S3].

#### *Plasmopara viticola* (Downy Mildew)

**Overwintering:**
- Survives exclusively as **oospores** (sexual, thick-walled resting spores) in infected fallen leaves and soil [S10, S14].
- Oospores formed by sexual reproduction (oogonia + antheridia) inside leaf tissue during growing season; released when leaves decompose [S9].
- Oospore germination is **episodic** and continues throughout the growing season (not depleted at bloom as formerly assumed) [S14].
- Oospore pool in leaf litter linked to severity of previous season's disease; higher inoculum dose → earlier DM onset [S42].

**Primary infection cycle:**
1. Rain splash + soil moisture triggers oospore germination → **macrosporangium** forms at germ-tube apex.
2. Macrosporangium releases zoospores (biflagellate, motile) into free water [S9].
3. Zoospores swim to stomata of lower leaf surface, encyst, form primary germ tube entering substomatal cavity [S43].
4. Intercellular hyphae proliferate; haustoria penetrate mesophyll cells for nutrient extraction [S43].
5. Latent period (no visible symptoms): **5–18 days** (temperature-dependent) [S16].
6. Sporangiophores emerge from stomata at night under moist conditions; **oil-spot** lesion visible on upper leaf surface; white "downy" sporulation on lower surface.

**Secondary infection cycle:**
1. Sporangiophores and sporangia produced **nocturnally** when: nighttime moist period ≥3 h + T 10–30°C [S9].
2. Sporangia dispersed by rain splash and wind during day; land on wet leaf surfaces.
3. Each sporangium releases 6–8 zoospores upon contact with free water; zoospores infect via stomata [S9].
4. Infection period: ≥2 h at T 21°C; longer at sub-optimal temperatures [S9].
5. Multiple secondary cycles per season; cycle time (sporulation → symptoms) as short as **4 days** at 23°C [S9].

**Latency and sporangia survival:**
- DM sporulation productivity: does not decline with lesion age in absence of sporulation-inducing weather [S14].
- After repeated sporulation cycles, lesion productivity declines rapidly [S14].
- Sporangia mortality during daytime in fair weather: **most sporangia die during daylight period** immediately following nocturnal production [S14].
- Sporangia survival under overcast conditions: **>50% viable after 12–24 h** [S14].
- Sporangia viability governed by VPD (Brischetto et al. 2021) — high VPD accelerates mortality of both attached and detached sporangia [S9].

**Ascospore vs. conidia comparison:**

| Feature | *E. necator* Ascospores | *E. necator* Conidia | *P. viticola* Oospores/Zoospores | *P. viticola* Sporangia/Zoospores |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Season | Spring (primary) | All season (secondary) | Spring–season (primary) | All season (secondary) |
| Trigger | Rain ≥2–3 mm + T >10°C | Temperature 21–30°C; wind | Rain + T >10°C + phenology | Night moisture + T 10–30°C |
| Dispersal | Wind | Wind | Rain splash | Rain + wind |
| Free water need | Required for release; not for germination | Detrimental | Required for germination | Required for zoospore release |
| Latent period | Variable (same as conidial) | 5–25 d (T-dependent) | 5–18 d | 5–18 d |
